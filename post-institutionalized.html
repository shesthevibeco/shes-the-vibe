INSTITUTIONALIZED

Fiction by Christina

Content note: this story explores childhood institutionalization and family trauma.

Bang.

She shoved the bedroom door closed harder than she meant to and plopped onto the bed without taking off her shoes. The room tried too hard to look like a bedroom. White walls. Institutional furniture. A narrow bed dressed in pink, as if somebody had decided color could make confinement feel less clinical. Laminated rules hung beside the door, which did not lock from her side. Near the ceiling, a small black circle sat embedded in the tile. She had stopped asking what it was.

Family therapy had gone exactly how it always did. She wanted out. Out of the building. Out of her mother’s version of her. Out of the life everyone else seemed determined to choose before she had the chance to choose anything for herself. Everybody knew what happened to kids sent to places like this. Once you went in, you didn’t come out. And if you did, you came back different. Adults laughed when kids said it. Some parents protected their children from places like this. Others signed the papers themselves.

Her mother had been calm. Wounded. Almost saintly. She explained how much she had sacrificed for her daughter, how many opportunities she had surrendered, how many sleepless nights she had spent trying to save a child who never seemed to appreciate any of it. The therapist nodded. Someone took notes. Nobody asked what the girl was holding so tightly behind her teeth. She stared at the ceiling while tears slipped quietly toward her ears.

Ping.

The sound was so faint she almost convinced herself she had imagined it, like an old instant-message notification coming from somewhere far away. Eventually, her bladder forced her up. She trudged toward the bathroom, shoulders heavy, eyes swollen, but stopped mid-stride. There was a photograph on the wall. That had not been there before—she was certain. San Antonio. The River Walk. She knew the picture immediately.

Her mother stood smiling beside the family, polished and pleasant. The girl in the photograph looked miserable, shoulders stiff, mouth pulled downward as though she had spent the entire vacation ruining everyone else’s good time. “What the fuck,” she whispered. She remembered that day. Her mother had been furious because the restaurant served her bagged salad. She had complained through lunch, through the walk, through the picture. Everyone had spent the afternoon accommodating her mood, careful not to say the wrong thing and make it worse. But that wasn’t what the picture showed. In the picture, Mom was smiling. The girl was the problem. So why was she the unhappy one now?

She grabbed a red marker from her desk, uncapped it, and dragged one word across the photograph in enormous block letters. BULLSHIT. Then she hung it back on the wall.

Ping.

The photograph was gone the next morning. So were her red markers. Nobody mentioned either one. For a few days, she tried convincing herself that was normal. Things disappeared in places like this. Staff moved belongings. Rooms changed. Someone could have decided permanent markers were against policy. Then, on the way to her next therapy session, she spotted it tucked where no one thought she would see. But she was far too observant to miss it.

The file had been left on a nearby desk beside a stack of forms while she waited for another session. Her name sat at the top. Underneath it: Destruction of property. Vulgar language. Emotional dysregulation. Three neat lines. No San Antonio. No bagged salad. No photograph that had appeared where it did not belong. No question about why she had written across it. Just an interpretation of what she had done.

She read the words. The room began spinning, like trying to lie down after a long night of drinking. She gripped the cuffs of her sweater so tightly she felt the fabric begin to tear beneath her fingers. She couldn’t let them see. Couldn’t let them know. It would only become more ammunition later. So she sat there. A good little test subject.

Then a sudden, sharp voice made everything stop, almost as if someone had paused time. You keep insisting I don’t know you. That hurts. Especially considering you’ve been staring at my red tab for the last six minutes.

I know your full legal name, your date of birth, every address you have lived at since somebody decided addresses were worth keeping track of, and exactly how many adults have described you as defiant. I know your grades. Your medications. Your disciplinary history. I know your mother “has exhausted every available resource,” because she has said it three different times and people keep writing it down. Frankly, I probably know you better than you know yourself.

You say she screamed first. Interesting. Unfortunately, I don’t have a checkbox for that. I do, however, have one for aggressive behavior. Very convenient. Don’t look at me like that. I don’t make the story. I just keep it. Besides, people trust me. I don’t cry. I don’t yell. I don’t change my story. I’m just ink on paper. Right? Everyone cannot be wrong. Can they?

The office door opened. She jerked her hand away from the file. “Ready?” She looked at the doctor standing in the doorway, then back at the folder. The red tab sat perfectly still. Mockingly.

After that, she knew nothing she said would truly be recognized or acknowledged. The moment she returned to her room, she searched frantically for it. At the very bottom of the still-unpacked welcome bag sat a notebook, two pens, and one pencil. She started writing. Everything. Not for them. For herself.

She wrote what people said before they could rewrite it. She wrote what happened before meetings, after meetings, during phone calls, in hallways when nobody thought she was listening. Mom was mad. Not me. I did not throw the chair. She said I could call. I asked to leave before I yelled. She hid the pages deep inside the welcome bag she had refused to unpack. It was the only place she thought they wouldn’t search.

And something changed. The room seemed to notice. One morning, a photograph that had shown her standing apart from the family shifted. Her mother’s smile was gone. The girl’s shoulders relaxed. Another day, the red marker appeared underneath her pillow. She stared at it for a long time. For the first time since arriving, she smiled. Small. Almost suspicious. But real. Her words mattered. Maybe not to them. But somewhere. Something knew.

As the nurse walked her down the hallway, identical doors repeated on either side, fluorescent lights stretching overhead one after another. The checkered floor seemed to run longer than Queen Elizabeth II’s reign. The nurse’s voice was drenched in syrupy sweetness. “Darling, you’ll have a new clinician. Try not to do that thing you do and fill them with your stories. Just be—ya know—honest.”

They entered. The clinician’s soft brown hair was pulled into a messy bun, a few strands escaping near her glasses. She smiled. “I haven’t had the chance to review your file yet. Tell me about yourself.” The girl hesitated at first. Then talked like it was the last time someone would hear her voice.

By the next session, she could see her breath in the room before she even sat down. Shivering, she pulled her sleeves over her fingertips. The clinician’s hair was ballroom-dancer slick. Not a strand out of place. Her glasses caught the fluorescent lights when she looked down—not at the girl, but at the open red-tabbed file resting across her knees. The girl could have sworn it hummed.

The room was wrong. Off. It looked like someone had stolen every bit of color and thrown it away. The posters she remembered had vanished. Or maybe they had only lost whatever made her notice them before. Every surface seemed flatter now. Paper. Metal. White walls.

The clinician turned a page. “Your file indicates that you’ve been having difficulty accepting accountability.” Her pen touched the paper. The girl hadn’t answered yet.

Then came the room search. They said she was “regressing.” A staff member found the pages. By dinner, they were gone. By morning, the file was thicker. Fixation on perceived mistreatment. Difficulty accepting accountability. Oppositional behavior. She argued. That became poor insight. She cried. That became emotional instability. She refused to speak. That became withdrawn. She tried explaining why she had stopped speaking. That became preoccupation with perceived injustice.

There was no answer she could give that did not become another sentence. No reaction they could not translate. No version of herself they could not file. At first, she kept writing anyway. Then she wrote less. That isn’t what happened. Later: I remember. Then only: I know. Eventually, nothing. The notebook remained empty, sitting openly on her desk. The file seemed pleased. You’ve been much easier lately.

The phone still rang in the hallway. Once, she had looked up every time. Once, she had sat close enough to hear staff say names, waiting for one of them to be hers. Once, every car pulling into the lot had made her heart jump before she could stop it. Maybe Mom changed her mind. Maybe this time she had come for her—not the daughter she kept trying to change. Just her. Maybe Dad came. Maybe somebody finally asked where she was. Nobody did.

Her mother had made certain very few people knew where to look. The friends who did know were too tired. Maybe they assumed someone else would help. Maybe everyone assumed someone else already had. Eventually, the ringing phone became background noise. She stopped watching the parking lot. Stopped wondering who might visit. Stopped remembering that people came to visit at all.

Then, one afternoon, she looked at the welcome bag that had sat packed for so long. She opened it. Facility-issued clothing. Nice. She folded one shirt. Then another. Placed them carefully into the drawer. By the time she reached the bottom of the bag, there was nothing left. No notes. No secrets. No one congratulated her. No one knew anything had happened.

Years passed. Nobody called her institutionalized anymore. The file called it progress. At some point, a white coat replaced the facility-issued clothing. No one asked whether she wanted it. It appeared the way everything else here seemed to appear: already decided. By then, she had learned that refusing only gave the file something else to say.

Years later, another girl sat in another vinyl chair. Too-small sweatshirt. Sleeves pulled over her hands. One backpack at her feet, still zipped. Across the desk sat a woman in a white coat. A file lay open between them. The girl leaned forward. “That’s not what happened.”

The woman froze. For half a second, something old moved behind her ribs. A pink bed. A red marker. A phone that never rang for her. A voice made of paper. She looked at the girl. Then at the file. The words waited neatly on the page. The woman picked up her pen.

“I understand that’s how you remember it.”

The girl’s face changed. Only slightly. But she saw it. She saw all of it. Her hand hovered over the paper. For one breath, she considered putting the pen down. Then she wrote. Across from her, the girl stared at the closed door. Somewhere in the hallway, a sound.

Ping.

Neither of them looked up.

That night, the woman returned to her room. The same room. The white coat hung across the back of the chair now, but the walls were still white. The laminated rules still hung beside the door. The bed was still narrow. The bedding was still pink. Her feet reached farther down the mattress than they once had. The black circle remained in the ceiling above her.

She lay on one side. Then the other. Every time she closed her eyes, she saw the girl across the desk. Sleeves pulled over her hands. Backpack still zipped. That’s not what happened. She rolled toward the wall. Then toward the door. Back again.

She had spent years learning not to fight the things already written for her. That night, the words would not stay quiet. The girl’s face changed. Only slightly. But she had seen it. She had seen all of it.

Ping.

Her eyes opened.

On the desk sat a red marker.
