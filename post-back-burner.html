Everything Gets Fed Before My Dream Does

A sick-day truth about building a business in the leftover hours

Everybody in my house is sick right now. Including me.

I’m the sickest one here, and I’m also the nurse. That’s the part nobody warns you about. There’s no sick leave from being the mom. No PTO bank. No calling in. The thermometer still comes to me. The medicine cups still come to me. A hundred little “mommy”s a day still come to me, because when a baby feels bad, he doesn’t want the concept of a parent. He wants his parent. He wants me.

My husband does amazing. I want that on the record before anybody twists this — he does everything he can, and he does it without being asked twice. But he is not the operating system of this house. I’m the one who knows which cough is the bad cough. Which kid needs the medicine cut with juice and which one will take it straight if you make it a race. Where the spare everything is. A household runs on a thousand pieces of information that live in exactly one person’s head, and in this house that head currently has a fever.

And here’s what I keep thinking about, laid up in the middle of all of it: this is the job. This is job one. Full-time doesn’t even cover it, because full-time jobs end. This one doesn’t clock out. It doesn’t have weekends. It doesn’t care that you’re sick, because the people who need you are sick too, and smaller.

Then there’s job two. The actual job. A hundred cases, a hundred other families’ worst days, sitting in my inbox waiting for me to be competent at them. That job gets second place in everybody’s expectation, including mine, and it takes it — loudly, on schedule, whether I’m well or not.

So do the math on where that leaves job three.

Job three is the business. The dream. The thing with my name on it. And the world has a very clear opinion about where it goes: last. After the kids, after the job, after the house, after everybody’s fed — literally and otherwise. It gets the back burner. It gets whatever is left of me, which some days is a woman on a couch at 10:47 at night, voice gone, laptop burning her legs, checking her shop numbers like a raccoon checking a trash can. One sale. Up from zero. Building an empire at the speed of a loading bar.

People love to ask women why we don’t just do more. Start the business. Chase the thing. As if the hours are in a drawer somewhere and we just haven’t opened it. Baby, the hours are not in a drawer. The hours are in a car seat. The hours are in a Wednesday meeting. The hours are in knowing where the Pedialyte is at 2 a.m. The dream doesn’t get scheduled. It gets smuggled — into lunch breaks, into the quiet after bedtime, into the forty-five minutes between one kid’s fever breaking and the next kid needing to be held.

And then the guilt shows up, right on time, like it pays rent. Because every hour I spend building is an hour I can account for somewhere else. I could’ve rested. I could’ve folded the laundry that’s currently forming its own civilization. I could’ve just been present, whatever that means when you’re present 25 hours a day already. Even rest feels like theft when you’re a mom building something. Like I’m stealing from my own kids to give to a website.

I want to say this part plain, because I need to hear it too:

The back burner still cooks.

Low and slow is still cooking. A pot on the back burner is not a pot you gave up on — it’s dinner that’s going to be ready because somebody kept it on the heat. My business is not a hobby I’m doing instead of mothering my kids. It is mothering my kids. It’s the exit plan. It’s the “my daughter will never have to watch me dim myself to keep a house peaceful” plan. It’s the reason there’s a welder in my craft room and journals with my name on them and a whole entire vision of my kids growing up watching their mama build something that was hers.

So no, it doesn’t get first place. It might never get first place. First place is taken by three small people who did not ask to be here and deserve every bit of it. Second place is taken by the job that keeps the lights on. But third place is not nothing. Third place, in leftover hours, with a fever, counts.

If you’re reading this from your own back burner — building in the margins, in the pickup line, on the couch with a laptop and a sick baby asleep on your chest — I need you to know you’re not behind. You’re not lazy. You’re not “not wanting it enough.” You are building an entire second life in the scraps of a day that was already two full-time jobs.

That’s not a small thing. That’s the whole vibe.

I’m going to go take my medicine now. The nurse has to keep herself alive — nobody else in this house knows the dosage.
